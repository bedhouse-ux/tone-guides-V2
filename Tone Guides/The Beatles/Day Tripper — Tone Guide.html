Tone Guide

Day Tripper

The Beatles — Single (1965)
Key: E Rig: Clapp 15 Feel: Bright, edge-of-breakup riff tone

The Reference Point

Nobody agrees on the exact guitar for this session — candidates range from a Gretsch to an ES-345 — but the Epiphone Casino is one of the most commonly cited choices, and it's genuinely the right family of instrument for the job: John, Paul, and George were all playing Casinos on stage by this point in 1965/66, and its hollow-body P90 bark suits this riff's bright, slightly gritty bite far better than a humbucker or a scooped single-coil would. The consensus on the amp tone itself is a bit more settled: a slightly edgy, not-quite-clean sound rather than a heavily fuzzed or distorted one — the riff should snap and cut, not roar.

Bridge dog-ear P90 for the bite and cut this riff needs — the neck pickup would round it off too much and lose some of the punch on the lower-string notes. Volume wide open so the amp gets a full, consistent signal to push against. Tone at 7 rather than fully open keeps the top end present without tipping into ice-picky; P90s can get harsh at full brightness, and this riff wants edge, not glare.

Same caution as the Thickfreakness guide — this guitar is fully hollow and genuinely resonant, so keep an eye on proximity to the amp as the gain and volume climb.

No pedals needed for this one — the tone comes entirely from the guitar pushing a moderately driven amp. Adding a drive pedal on top tends to push this past the "edgy clean" character into something closer to a modern crunch tone, which loses the mid-60s British Invasion feel this riff is built on.

Class A mode is the right call here for a reason different from the fuzz-driven Zeppelin builds — it's not about the sag, it's that its earlier breakup gets you to that "edge of clean" character at a more usable volume than pushing the full 15W A/B into breakup would. Bright switch on plus a treble-forward EQ for the snap and top-end presence this riff wants. Pre-Amp Gain set to a genuine middle ground: enough that picking hard on the riff gives you a bit of grit and bark, light enough that chording through it in the chorus doesn't turn to mush. Bass Defeat stays off — the 3-band EQ here is doing useful work keeping the low end controlled rather than boomy on a hollow-body guitar.
