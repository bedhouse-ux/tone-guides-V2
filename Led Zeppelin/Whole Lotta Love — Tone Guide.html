Tone Guide

Whole Lotta Love

Led Zeppelin — Led Zeppelin II (1969)
Key: E Rig: Clapp 15 Feel: Fuzz-driven blues riff

The Reference Point

Jimmy Page cut the main riff through a Telecaster into a Tone Bender-style fuzz, running into a small combo — a compact, aggressive, mid-forward growl rather than a wall of Marshall stack gain. The goal here is that same character: a fuzz doing the heavy lifting, a Class-A combo doing the breathing, and a bridge single-coil keeping the riff articulate instead of mushy.

Postmodern Telecaster

8
Tone

Bridge position for the Nocaster '51 pickup: tight low end, clear balanced midrange, classic twangy voicing — the closest thing in the collection to Page's vintage Tele bridge tone, and it gives the fuzz something articulate to chew on. Volume all the way up, since the fuzz is doing the gain work and you want a full, consistent signal feeding it. Tone just barely rolled off from full (8, not 10) to knock a little edge off the top end before it hits the fuzz — full open can get a bit glassy/thin through this particular circuit. For the solo, roll Tone down further, toward 5–6, for a rounder, more vocal lead tone that separates it from the riff.

The Warm Bender lives on the shelf, not the board — pull it in front for this one, and place it right after the tuner, ahead of the Mira. Fuzz circuits like this are sensitive to what's in front of them; putting a compressor or buffer before it can choke the fuzz's interaction with your guitar's volume knob (see the Mira note below). The pedal is currently listed as new/in transit, so double check it's landed before you build this rig.

Pedals

Not Used

Walrus Audio Mira

Bypassed for this song.

Sits between the tuner and the fuzz in the board's normal order, so it's worth calling out explicitly: leave it off. Compressing ahead of a germanium fuzz like the Warm Bender evens out your picking dynamics before the fuzz ever sees them, which works against the riff — part of what makes this riff snarl is the fuzz reacting unevenly to pick attack. If you want compression later for a cleaner rhythm section elsewhere in a set, this is the pedal, but not here.

Main Drive

Warm Audio Warm Bender

Version: NOS 75
Sag: off
7
Attack
6
Level

This is the riff's engine. The Warm Bender's Version rotary picks between three circuits — NOS 76 (cutting, present, two-transistor), NOS 75 (Mark II Professional-style, more bass and warmth), and a modern silicon mode. NOS 75 is the one, since it's the mode Warm Audio specifically voices toward the Cream/Zeppelin-style fuzz tone. Attack set high for the wall-of-fuzz riff sound — this is the fuzz/gain amount, not output level. Level around unity so the fuzz doesn't jump the mix on its own; the Tumnus handles any extra push for the solo. Sag left off for the main riff — it starves the circuit for a compressed, "dying battery" feel, which is a great option to try for the solo but works against the tight low end the riff wants.

This pedal's controls (Version / Attack / Level / Sag) aren't listed in the rig reference file — I pulled them from Warm Audio's own product documentation since the reference only notes the three circuit modes. Worth adding the full control set to the file if this checks out against the actual unit.

Gain kept low — on this circuit, turning gain up also pulls back a parallel clean-blend, so a low setting favors that clean blend and reads more as a volume/sustain push than added dirt. Volume above unity to give the solo a lift over the fuzz. EQ left flat/centered so it doesn't reshape the fuzz's voicing, just adds behind it. Normal mode, not Hot, keeps this a boost rather than a second gain stage.

Spring-style voicing, decay kept short and predelay near-zero — enough to keep the amp from sounding dry and boxy, not audible as "reverb." Keep the Starlight Echo and Brigade off; this riff wants to stay dry and direct.

There's no wah in the current collection — Page reached for one elsewhere in the Zeppelin catalog and in the freakout midsection of this track, but the main riff and solo don't need it, so it's not a gap for this particular guide.

Class A mode gives you the earlier breakup and sag of a small vintage combo — closer to what the fuzz was originally paired with than the full 15W A/B headroom. Mid pushed forward keeps the riff cutting rather than scooped; Bass Defeat stays off since the fuzz already supplies plenty of low end and the 3-band EQ is doing useful shaping work here. Bright switch off — the fuzz and bridge pickup already have enough top end, no need to stack more.

If you're playing with a full band and need more headroom, flip to Class A/B and nudge Master up to compensate — the tone character holds, you just get more clean power on tap before the amp itself starts sagging.
