Tone Guide

The Runner

Foals — Everything Not Saved Will Be Lost – Part 2 (2019)
Key: D minor (Drop D tuning) Rig: Clapp 15 Feel: Fuzzy, prowling verse riff; synth-textured chorus and bridge

The Reference Point

DIY Magazine's Lisa Wright described the track as "swaggering straight in with guitars that prowl," with "something more fallible, more grandiose that sweeps in as the chorus arrives." Album of the Year's review fills in what that shift actually is: rocky, guitar-driven verses expanding into "open reverb-tinged synths" for the chorus — guitar and that synth texture only combine in the track's two short bridges, where there's a guitar solo over the same synth bed.

That's the structural insight behind this revision. There's no synth in the collection, so rather than treating the chorus as "guitar but bigger," the Luminary V2's octave-stacking stands in for the synth expansion the record actually reaches for — the same gap-filling logic as the EB-10's fixed-wah approximation on Immigrant Song. The verse stays exactly what it was: a fuzz-driven, articulate riff. The chorus and bridges are where the rework happens.

The Luminary sits ahead of the fuzz on purpose — pitch/octave tracking reads the clean waveform to figure out what's being played, and fuzz clips and compresses that waveform hard, which degrades tracking (more of a problem for a polyphonic tracker than a simple monophonic one). It needs to see the signal before the Swollen Pickle touches it.
This is now a three-state song rather than two: Verse — Swollen Pickle on, Luminary bypassed, same prowling riff as before. Chorus — Swollen Pickle off, Luminary's Preset 1 recalled, guitar pulled back to let the octave layer carry the "synth" expansion. Bridge (guitar solo) — Swollen Pickle off, Luminary's Preset 2 recalled, a fuller blend since this is the one section where the record actually layers guitar over synths together. The EB-10's cut stays on throughout — no switching needed there.

Scoop left off — this riff needs midrange presence to stay cutting and jagged rather than the huge, flat power-chord voicing Scoop gives you, which would work against the Firebird's already-bright character. Crunch engaged for tighter compression, keeping the riff defined rather than a loose wall of Muff-style saturation.

What's new: this pedal now switches off entirely for the chorus and bridges, rather than staying engaged under everything. The fuzz's job is the verse's prowl — once the arrangement opens up into synth territory, keeping it on would just be a louder riff, not the textural shift the record makes.

Walrus Audio Luminary V2

+1 leads, giving the shimmer and lift the "grandiose" chorus wants; a lighter -1 underneath adds body without turning muddy. Dry/Wet kept modest at 3 — this is meant to sit alongside the dry guitar as a textural layer, not replace it. Attack at 5 gives the octaves a gradual bloom rather than snapping in with the pick attack, closer to how a synth pad swells than how a guitar note does. Filter at 6 rounds off the top of the octave signal so it reads as pad-like rather than digital or glassy. Flutter kept low (2) for a touch of movement without it reading as an obvious tremolo.

Same voicing as the chorus preset — Attack, Filter, and Flutter unchanged — just with Dry/Wet pushed up to around 5.5 and -1 nudged slightly hotter. This is the one section where guitar and synth texture genuinely coexist on the record, so the octave layer is allowed to come forward more than it does under the chorus vocal.

Storing a preset on the Luminary: dial in the sound in Live mode, then use the press-and-hold save gesture (per the pedal's manual — worth a quick check against the actual unit once it's landed, since the exact button combo isn't listed in the rig reference) to write it to Preset 1 or 2. Recall is then a single tap of the preset footswitch during the song, no knob-turning required. Preset 3 is left open for future songs.

A different job for this pedal than its usual fixed-wah role (see Immigrant Song). Here it's pure cleanup: the Luminary's -1 octave, stacked on Drop D's already-low open string, can get flubby heading into the amp. Low's toggle flipped to the Cut side and its 9-position knob turned up a few steps — not maxed — trims a solid chunk of bottom end so the chorus/bridge texture stays full without turning to mud. Walrus doesn't publish an exact dB-per-step figure for the knob, so treat this as "cut noticeably, not extremely" rather than a precise number until you can dial it in by ear against the actual pedal. Mid and High left at 0 (flat, no cut or boost) — no additional tonal shaping, just correction. This stays engaged throughout, including the verse — it's not doing anything audible when the Luminary's bypassed, so there's no need to switch it separately.

Verse decay stays short and subtle at 1.5 — this is a modern studio-rock riff, not a wash, and the original review's "reverb-tinged" language is specifically about the chorus. Push Decay up to around 3 for the chorus and bridges, leaning into that description and giving the Luminary's octave layer somewhere to bloom, closer to the open, spacious quality of an actual synth pad.

Amp settings are unchanged and stay fixed across all three sections — Class A/B for punch and tightness under the fuzz in the verse; Mid pushed forward and Bright on to keep things present and cutting. With the fuzz bypassed for the chorus and bridges, the amp is now the main color on the guitar signal feeding the Luminary — its own EL84 breakup adds a bit of warmth to the octave layer rather than leaving it clinically clean. If the chorus/bridge sound feels like it's fighting the reverb wash for space, roll the guitar's Volume knob back slightly rather than touching the amp — keeps the transition consistent without adding another thing to switch.
